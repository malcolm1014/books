PAHRUMP, NV/FOUR TWELVES/A LEVEL VOICE/ONE FORM LETTER
CHAPTER ONE

Sunday Gravy on a Friday

The gravy went on at six in the morning because Shabbat started at seven-forty that night and Lilian Marchetti Brandt had never once in her life been late to her own table. She browned the pork neck bones in the bottom of Nonna Concetta's fourteen-quart pot, the one with the scorch ring baked permanently into the outside, the one her grandmother had earned across forty years in a kitchen two blocks off Fisherman's Wharf in Monterey that smelled of squid and diesel and bay laurel and never once smelled of anything else. Sicilians in her family did not cook Sunday gravy on a Friday. Sicilians in her family also did not braid challah and light two candles and say a blessing over them naming Yeshua, so as far as Lilian was concerned she had already broken the rules that mattered and everything after that was scheduling.

She had come to it at twenty-six, alone, out of a Bible she bought at a thrift store in Tonopah for a dollar-fifty, and it had cost her most of her family and all of her parish and she would have paid double. Her mother still sent Christmas cards addressed to Mrs. Dale Brandt with a little gold cross sticker sealing the flap, which Lilian understood was not a greeting but a position paper. She braided the dough at six-forty with Ruthie standing on a step stool beside her, seven years old and furious about it, insisting she got the middle strand because the middle strand was the important one. Lilian let her have it. There is no important strand. That is the entire structural principle of a braid, that no single piece of it holds anything up by itself, and Lilian figured her daughter had about thirty years to work that out and there was no sense rushing her.

Marisol came down at seven, fourteen years old with her great-grandmother's exact face and a phone she was not supposed to have. Antonio was eleven and could name every locomotive class that ran the Union Pacific line through Ludlow, and did, at length, to anyone. Bennie was four and climbed into her lap like it was a chair that belonged to him, which, in the only ledger that has ever mattered, it did.

Pahrump, Nevada, sits sixty-two miles from Las Vegas and roughly four hundred years from it, a wide dirt valley of chain-link and gravel lots and creosote that goes the color of an old paper bag by the second week of April. They had a half acre off Manse Road, a double-wide with a stick-built addition Dale put on himself the summer Bennie was born, four dogs of no describable breed, a swamp cooler on the roof that sounded like a helicopter landing badly, and a view of the Spring Mountains that on a clear cold morning could stop her flat in the driveway with a laundry basket on her hip and her breath going up white in front of her. She had come out here at twenty-three because Dale got on with the mine in Amargosa Valley and the money was real money with a pension attached, and she was a girl from a fishing family in a town that had run out of fish, so she knew precisely what real money was worth.

He worked four twelves and got three off, and she ran the entire household on that clock the way her uncles had run a boat. Breakfast at six-fifteen. Lunch pail with two sandwiches, no crust, cut on the diagonal, because he had remarked once in 2014 that squares were for children. Truck warmed in winter. Coffee going before the alarm on his side ever sounded, because he liked to come out of the bedroom and find it made and he did not like to come out and find it not made, and there existed no version of that morning in which anybody won. She had the sequence timed to under nine minutes and could run it in the dark and routinely did. And what he brought home at seven-forty at night he set down on her, in a completely level voice, with the television going: that she had gone thick through the middle and it was a shame because she used to be something; that Marisol was mouthy because her mother had no spine and never had; that she was lucky, genuinely lucky, that he was the kind of man who stayed, because who exactly did she imagine was lining up; that the church people had gotten into her head and her Jewish thing was a costume she wore to make herself interesting and her grandmother would be ashamed of her.

That was the genius of the level voice. A raised voice is evidence. A level voice is just a Tuesday. And Lilian, standing at the sink with her hands in the water, running the arithmetic her uncles had run in the dark on a boat taking water, had understood two things completely for about a year and a half: that it was not going to stop, because it had not stopped for a new baby or for the year he got sober or for the counselor in Vegas who lasted three sessions; and that it was going to move from his mouth to his hands on a timeline she did not control, most likely on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays the mine ran safety reviews and he came home already carrying something he needed to set down on somebody.

CHAPTER TWO

The Blog

It began, as these things do, with a form letter, which is the least romantic opening in recorded history. The Nye County School District had been breached in February: names, addresses, dates of birth, parent contact information, and for a subset of families the free-and-reduced-lunch paperwork, which is to say income. The letter offered twelve months of credit monitoring and used the phrase out of an abundance of caution twice on a single page. Lilian read it at the mailbox in flip-flops at four in the afternoon in a hundred and one degrees, and the thing that snagged on her was not the credit monitoring and was not the money. It was one sentence in the third paragraph saying the intrusion had begun in November and had not been detected until February.

Somebody had been standing inside a building that held her children's names for three months and nobody noticed. She stood in the gravel with the mail against her chest and thought, with something close to personal offense, how do you not notice for three months. That night she typed the question into Google the way she typed everything, badly, at 1:12 in the morning, on a 2016 HP Pavilion with a swelling battery and eleven browser toolbars, sitting on the closed lid of the toilet in the hall bathroom because it was the only room in the house with a door that locked and an outlet. She searched how do hackers get into a school district. Two hours later she was somewhere she had not meant to go, having traveled from a local news item to a wire story to a blog by a man named Brian Krebs who apparently just did this, constantly, in plain English, with receipts.

She read about ransomware crews that ran helpdesks with posted business hours. She read about an intrusion that started at a vendor whose remote access tool still carried a default password set in 2011. She read a piece on SIM swapping four times through and could have taught it by dawn. And somewhere in there she understood something about herself that reorganized her whole interior furniture, which was that she had spent twenty years developing what her cousin Gina called Google Fu, for entirely domestic reasons and to a professional edge nobody had ever thought to call professional: finding the cheapest tires across three counties, running down an out-of-print Sicilian cookbook printed in Palermo in 1961, determining at nine on a Sunday which urgent care took their garbage insurance for a split lip.

She knew how to read a page of results and tell inside four seconds who actually knew and who was performing knowing. She knew how to take one question and reframe it five ways until the internet finally gave up what it was holding. She knew the answer to a hard question usually sits inside a document written for a completely different purpose, and that you find it by asking who would have been required to write it down. That was not a housewife skill. That was the skill, and it had a name, and an ethics, and conference tracks, and a labor market, in a city she could see from her own driveway on a clear day.

Four nights later she found the podcast, one episode, downloaded over library wifi because their data plan was two gigabytes and metered and, she was fairly sure, reviewed. A woman whose ex-husband had put an application on her phone and read every message she sent for two years while telling her she was imagining things. Lilian listened to all of it in the van in the Smith's parking lot with the air conditioning running and her hands flat on her thighs and her groceries going soft in the back, and when it ended she sat through four minutes of silence and then played the last nine minutes again. Then she went inside, bought milk she did not need, came back out, and opened her own phone. Settings. General. Then down, and down, into the rooms of a phone that nobody is ever meant to enter, and there it was: an account she had never created, a device management profile she had never approved, location sharing whose toggle was greyed out and unavailable because the switch for it did not live on her end of the line.

Her stomach dropped and then went very still and very quiet, the way the deck of a boat goes quiet when the engine finally cuts and you can hear what is actually out there in the dark with you. Eleven years of being told she was paranoid, that she was dramatic, that nobody was doing anything to her and she was doing it to herself, and here was a screen with a fact on it, and the fact did not care how she felt and could not be argued with and would not apologize later. She put the phone back in the cupholder face up, exactly where it had been sitting, at exactly the angle it had been sitting. She changed nothing. She cleared nothing. She did not so much as close the settings app, because a closed app is a decision and an open one is a woman who got distracted by a kid.

She drove home and browned onions and met him at the door at seven-forty with the face she had been building since 2011, and somewhere behind that wall, in a voice that sounded very much like her grandmother's, she thought: all right. Now I know what you can see.

CHAPTER THREE

Bandit

The Pahrump Community Library on East Basin Avenue had eleven public terminals, free wifi, a teen room with a beanbag, and a librarian named Faye who had worked there nineteen years and had developed the specific professional courtesy of not asking questions. Lilian got a card in her maiden name, because her maiden name was still printed on an expired passport and the form said any government-issued ID, and she walked back out into the heat with a piece of plastic that read L. MARCHETTI and felt like she had stolen a car. She started going Tuesdays and Thursdays, ten to eleven-thirty, while Bennie was at the church preschool, and she told anyone who asked that she was doing genealogy, which was even partly true; there was a folder in that account about the boats out of Monterey, about the Stella Maris that her great-grandfather ran out of the wharf with a crew of six cousins and one man from Trapani nobody liked.

What she was actually doing was OverTheWire. Bandit is a wargame, thirty-some levels, and Level 0 asks exactly one thing of you: connect to a server over SSH with the username and password they hand you, and go find the file that holds the next password. That is all it is. It took her ninety minutes and she had to look up what a terminal was, and what SSH was, and then what a shell was, and then she had to find out that the reason her password was not working was that the tutorial had wrapped a line and she had been typing a character that was not there. Then she got in. She typed ls, and a filename appeared.

She typed cat readme, and a string of characters came back. And Lilian Marchetti Brandt sat in a plastic chair in a public library in the Mojave Desert and cried over a password puzzle, quietly, with her hand over her mouth, because for the first time in eleven years she had asked something a direct question and received a direct and complete answer that had nothing whatsoever to do with what mood anybody was in. Machines do not gaslight. A machine will tell you exactly what you did wrong, every time, in the same words, forever, and it will never once mention your weight. She would have paid money for that.

She was, in fact, about to.

By week five she had ls, cd, cat, grep, find, file, du, and man, and she had learned the actual lesson underneath all of it, which is that the answer to how do I do this is almost always man followed by reading, patiently, the way she read a recipe written in dialect, three times through before touching a pan.

By week nine she was up in the twenties and had gotten sideways into SSH keys and nc and lost an entire Thursday to base64 because she could not initially accept that encoding and encryption were two different words describing two different things, and once she got it she was faintly angry about how many news articles she had read that got it wrong. She kept notes in a marbled composition book, the ninety-nine-cent kind, in the same tight upright hand she used for grocery lists, and the composition book lived in the bottom of a Rubbermaid tote in the shed underneath a forty-pound bag of dog food, because in eleven years Dale had never once lifted a bag of dog food.

The hardware came that September and it came the way everything came, in cash, in pieces, over months. She sold four of Nonna's copper pots to an antique dealer out in Tecopa for four hundred dollars and cried about it in the car for six minutes in the dirt lot and then put the car in gear and got over it. A refurbished Lenovo ThinkPad X220 off a man in Henderson for sixty-five dollars: i5, eight gigabytes, a keyboard she would have married, and one dead pixel in the upper left that she came to think of fondly as a mole on somebody's face. A sixteen-gigabyte SanDisk she turned into a Tails stick, which meant the laptop's own drive never mattered, because the entire operating system lived in memory and evaporated the instant she pulled the plug, and a laptop that remembers nothing is a laptop that cannot testify.

A Pinecil soldering iron, thirty dollars, and a spool of 63/37 rosin core, because a woman on a forum had written if you can't solder you will always be renting somebody else's hardware, and Lilian had never in her life liked renting anything. She burned her thumb badly the first night, learned to tin the tip, practiced on a dead Roku and then on a dead thermostat and then on a strip of perfboard, and by Sukkot she could lay a clean joint on a 0.1-inch header. The whole kit lived in a Christmas tin on the shelf above the water heater, labeled in marker: DECORATIONS — BROKEN.

CHAPTER FOUR

Stella Maris

She lurked four months before she typed a single word in public, which was deliberate, and which was the single smartest thing she did that year, because the security community has a very specific and very fast immune response to people who arrive loud. She read r/AskNetsec and r/netsec until she could feel the difference between the two rooms without looking at the URL. She got onto infosec.exchange and read Mastodon threads written by people whose job titles she had to look up, and she watched how they handled each other: the jokes, the ferocious and joyful pedantry about words, the way somebody would spend nine consecutive posts correcting a stranger's use of the word hacker and then offer to buy that stranger a beer in August.

She noticed the currency was not confidence, which was a relief, because she had none. The currency was did you actually try it. Nobody in that world cared who her husband was, or what she weighed, or that she had no degree; they cared whether she had read the man page before she opened her mouth. So when she finally posted, her first post was a question so well-constructed that three separate people asked what her background was. She had an eleven-dollar Ralink dongle that refused to go into monitor mode, and instead of help doesnt work plz she posted the exact adapter, the exact chipset out of lsusb, the kernel version, the three things she had already tried and their exact outputs, and the exact error, verbatim, in a code block.

A woman answered inside nine minutes: Your chipset is the problem, not you. Put it in a drawer and get an Alfa AWUS036NHA, the AR9271, it's ancient and it just works. Also this is a genuinely good bug report — do you do this professionally? Lilian read that last sentence something like forty times. She typed no. She deleted it. She typed not yet. Her handle was stellamaris, and within a month people were calling her Stell, and she found she liked being called Stell more than she had liked being called anything in a decade.

The woman was Deborah Achebe, who went by Sparrow, who ran a security operations team on the night side for a managed detection shop in Henderson and helped organize DC702, the Las Vegas DEF CON group that met the second Friday of the month at a bar off Sahara. Through Sparrow came Misha Volkov up in Reno, a hardware man with a bench full of ESP32s and a Bus Pirate and a Rigol scope, who talked to Lilian for two straight hours the first night about thermal mass and flux chemistry and never once asked why a mother of four in Pahrump wanted to know how to reball a chip. Through the boards came Trish Nakamura-Boyle, twenty years Air Force and now doing governance work for a hospital system, who reviewed strangers' resumes for free and held strong opinions about the word synergy. Lilian talked to all of them on a laptop that forgot everything the moment she unplugged it, in a locked bathroom, between eleven at night and two in the morning, and it was the best year of conversation she had had since 2011 and it happened entirely in text with people who had never seen her face.

Then two things happened in the same week of March. The first was that Sparrow mentioned offhandedly that DEF CON ran the second weekend of August at the Las Vegas Convention Center, that badges were cash at the door and no name attached, and that there was an entire children's convention inside it called r00tz Asylum where kids learned to solder and pick locks and were treated like people. Lilian pulled up the calendar on the wall. Dale's swing put him on four twelves Thursday through Sunday of exactly that week. The second thing happened on a Tuesday nine days later, over a cable bill, when he put his fist through the drywall eleven inches to the left of her head, and then apologized, and then explained that she had made him do it, and then said the thing he always said, which was that she would never be enough for anybody and ought to be grateful.

Marisol was standing in the hallway. Marisol saw all of it. And Lilian, holding a dish towel, looking at a hole in her own wall, felt the last piece of hope she had been carrying around like a stone in her shoe finally go quiet and stop hurting. She patched the drywall herself on Thursday and painted it Saturday and it was invisible by Sunday. She photographed it first, from four angles, with a tape measure in the frame, and uploaded the photographs to an account that did not exist as far as anyone in that house knew.

CHAPTER FIVE

Eight Hundred and Sixty Dollars

She had eight hundred and sixty dollars in a coffee tin and it had taken her fourteen months to build it. Twenty here from returning something she had never opened. Forty there from cash-back on a Smith's run she rounded up and pocketed. A hundred and ten for a set of dishes she sold on a Pahrump swap page and told him had broken in the move to the new cabinet, and he had called her clumsy for three days, and she had taken that as the price of a hundred and ten dollars, because that was the exchange rate and she had gotten very good at doing that math instantly. She hated the lying. She had been raised by women for whom a lie was a moral event with a smell to it, and she took it to God about it more than once at one in the morning on the bathroom floor, and what came back was not exactly absolution but something more like permission, the way you would give a woman permission to break a window in a burning house.

Four hundred and sixty of the tin went to a badge. The remaining four hundred went to two nights at a motor inn off Boulder Highway with an outdoor corridor and a pool the color of weak tea, four tanks of gas, and pizza. They left Pahrump at six-fifteen on a Thursday morning in August in a 2009 Sienna with a hundred and ninety-one thousand miles on it and one hubcap. She had told Dale the kids had a thing at the Springs Preserve, which was a real place and a plausible thing and cost nine dollars for children. She had told the kids the truth, all four of them, because Marisol was fourteen and not stupid, and because Lilian had made a decision somewhere in the previous winter that whatever else her children came out of that house carrying, it was not going to be the discovery that their mother lied to them.

Route 160 climbs out of the valley and over Mountain Springs Summit at nearly six thousand feet and then drops into the Las Vegas basin all at once, the whole grid of it laid out below like something spilled, and Bennie hollered about it, and Antonio read the elevation signs aloud in ascending order, and Ruthie slept with her mouth open, and Marisol sat up front with her feet on the dash and said, without looking over, are we ever going to live somewhere else, and Lilian said, yes. That was the entire conversation. It held for the rest of the year.

The line into the Las Vegas Convention Center West Hall is called Linecon by people who have been standing in it for a decade, and it was the friendliest hour of Lilian's adult life. She stood there with a four-year-old on her hip in a hall the size of an airfield, surrounded by black shirts and improbable hair and one man in a full ghillie suit, and a total stranger with a hand-soldered blinking badge around his neck gave Bennie a sticker and then spent twenty minutes crouched down showing Antonio how the ESP32 on that badge was quietly talking to every other badge in the room over wifi, drawing a mesh on the back of a napkin with a pen he then gave to the kid. Nobody asked Lilian what she did. Two people asked what she was into, which is an entirely different question with an entirely different door behind it, and when she said she was nine levels from the end of Bandit and had finally gotten monitor mode working on an AWUS036NHA, a woman ahead of her in line turned all the way around and said, oh, you're one of us. Cash at the window, no ID, no name in a database, a badge that was a physical object with actual electronics on it that she hung around her neck and touched about forty times that first hour like a woman checking for a wallet. r00tz Asylum took Antonio and Ruthie into a room where a volunteer put a soldering iron into an eleven-year-old's hand and taught him the same thing his mother had taught herself in a shed off Manse Road, and Antonio came out three hours later carrying a blinking circuit board he had built with his own two hands and did not put it down for the remainder of the trip, including in the bath, where it survived.

Marisol got recruited into a beginner capture-the-flag for teenagers and by hour two had beaten two adults at it and had been told so, out loud, in front of people. When she came out of that room her chin sat differently on her neck, and it never went back. That was the moment Lilian understood what this trip actually was, and it was not a convention. She had driven her children over a mountain so they could stand in a room where their mother was a person who belonged, once, for three days, and see it with their own eyes, and never afterward be entirely able to unsee it.

CHAPTER SIX

The Door

The Diana Initiative ran alongside the big con and had a completely different weather system to it, quieter, hotel conference rooms instead of an aircraft hangar, and a mission printed right on the program: to support and fast-track people underrepresented in information security. In practice that meant a room full of women and nonbinary folks and career-changers and one retired postal inspector, every one of whom had been informed at some point that this was not for them. Lilian wanted exactly one thing off that schedule, an eleven o'clock workshop called From Zero to SOC: Building a Path When You Have No Path, and she got there at ten-twenty, and it was already at capacity, and a volunteer in a red shirt was standing at the door with a clipboard and an apologetic face, and there were nineteen people in that hallway who were not getting in.

Taped to the door at eye level was a single sheet of paper. On it was a block of capital letters, and above the block somebody had written in Sharpie: SEATS HELD FOR THOSE WHO ASK NICELY. THE PASSPHRASE IS ON THIS PAGE. It was obviously a joke. Everyone in the hallway had glanced at it and gone back to their phones, because it was obviously a joke, some organizer's little gag from years back. Lilian looked at it for eleven seconds. Ciphertext, not a hash, wrong character distribution for a simple substitution, repeating trigrams roughly six apart. Vigenère, and the key was going to be short and it was going to be in her hand or on the wall, because a joke on a door is not meant to be unsolvable.

She checked her badge. She checked the program. She checked the door itself, and there was the little brass plate reading SALON C, and there on the lanyard around her own neck was the name of the convention, and she wrote DIANA in the margin of the program with a golf pencil and did the shift by hand on the back page while the woman beside her said, wait — are you actually doing it? It took her six minutes and she got it wrong twice because she kept forgetting that I and J share a cell, which is a thing you only ever learn by doing it wrong. The plaintext, when it came up out of the letters, said: ASK FOR ARTEMIS AND TELL HER YOU READ THE DOOR. Lilian folded the program, walked up to the volunteer, and said in the voice she used on insurance phone lines, I'm sorry to bother you. Is Artemis around? I read the door. The volunteer's entire face changed.

She keyed her radio, said four words into it, listened, and then held the door open. Lilian went into that room, and so did the woman who had been standing next to her, because Lilian stopped in the doorway, turned around, and said, she's with me, which was a lie, and the only one she has ever been proud of.

Artemis was Trish Nakamura-Boyle, who ran that track and had taped the page up herself at seven that morning as a joke that had gone unsolved for three years. Afterward she sat Lilian down with a paper plate of conference cookies and conducted a resume review on a woman who did not have a resume, and when she asked what Lilian had actually done and Lilian said, honestly, that she had a composition book, Trish said, show me the composition book. She read four pages of it and went very quiet. Then she said three things that reorganized the next two years. One: go to Western Governors, it is online and competency-based and flat-rate for a six-month term, you move as fast as you can move, and the cybersecurity degree has CompTIA certifications built into the coursework, so the paper and the certs come out of the same tuition.

Two: nobody in Las Vegas cares about your gap years, the SOC market here is starving, and this town hires on aptitude and shift availability. Three, written on a napkin along with a phone number: whatever is happening at home, there are people at this con who do that too, and you should talk to them before you do anything else. That afternoon Lilian wandered into the Trace Labs Search Party, which is a capture-the-flag where the flags are not invented — real missing-persons cases, submitted by families, vetted, judged on documented open-source findings, with an iron rule that you never contact the subject or the family or anyone near them. She got put on a four-person team because she was standing in the right hallway: a Marine veteran named Kwame, a nineteen-year-old from Tempe who typed like the keyboard owed him money, and an exhausted man from Ottawa named Pieter who did nothing but write the reports and turned out to be the most valuable human being on that floor.

Their case was a twenty-two-year-old woman, fourteen months gone from a town in the Central Valley, last seen at a gas station. The others ran the standard playbook, usernames and archived profiles and vehicle records, and Lilian did the thing she had been doing at kitchen tables her entire life, which was read people. She pulled the grandmother's 2019 obituary, got the name of the parish out of it, and went looking for parish content, and eleven seconds into a stranger's video of a church rummage sale she found a forearm at the edge of frame with three small birds tattooed on the inside of it, matching the intake sheet, which nobody else on that floor had found because nobody else on that floor was watching church rummage sale footage. That is not a tool and it is not a script.

That is a woman who understood that people go home, and that home for some people is a building with a saint's name on it. Pieter geolocated the lot off a light pole and a mural, timestamped it, sourced it, and submitted it, and the judge gave them a hundred and fifty points and then walked back over to ask who found it. They placed fourth and Lilian did not care about fourth. What she cared about happened at eleven-forty that night in a corridor beside a bank of dead escalators, when Sparrow, whom she had never met in person and recognized instantly, handed her a beer she did not drink and said, okay. You're obviously good at this. So what's the thing you're not saying? And Lilian, who had smiled through a thousand Tuesdays, stood in a convention center and said it out loud for the first time in eleven years, and it took four minutes.

Sparrow did not gasp and did not touch her and did not say why don't you just leave. She said, okay. Then she said, how much can he see? And when Lilian said, everything on my phone, and I think there's a tag in the van, Sparrow nodded slowly and said the sentence that became the spine of the following ten months: Then don't turn anything off. Not one thing. You leave every bit of it exactly the way he expects it to look, and you build the real life somewhere he isn't looking. Twenty minutes later a person who went by Hex found them by the escalators, handed Lilian a card with nothing on it but a URL for Operation Safe Escape, and said, we're a nonprofit, it's free, we do this specifically for people like you, and about half of us have been you.

SELF-MADE · SHE WROTE THE COMPILER

CHAPTER SEVEN

Two Lives

She came home Sunday night with a con badge, a solder burn on her left index finger, four children who would not stop talking about lockpicking, and an operating doctrine. The doctrine was this: there exists a Lilian that Dale is able to observe, and that Lilian is going to become more consistent than she has ever been, not less, because the way people get caught is that their cover story improves suddenly. So the family-managed phone stayed exactly as it was, in her pocket, location on, texting her sister about coupons and her aunt about a christening. The browser history on it stayed boring and stayed populated, because an empty history is a scream. She kept going to the same church, the same Smith's, the same preschool pickup, and she found the tracker in the van the following Wednesday in nine minutes, an Apple tag wedged up behind the spare tire cover in the well under the third row, and she sat in the driveway holding it in her palm and felt the exact shape of the temptation to throw it into the desert.

She put it back. Removing it would have told him more in one second than she was prepared to give him in a year, and she had worked out by then that surveillance you know about is not a cage, it is a channel, and a channel can be fed. So she fed it. She learned the tag was there and then built a life the tag would agree with: groceries on Monday, church Wednesday and Saturday, preschool five mornings, and the library, which happened to be on the way to the groceries, which is precisely why the library had been chosen fourteen months earlier by a woman who had not yet learned any of this and had simply been careful by instinct. She wrote her patterns down and kept them. She did not deviate. For ten months she was the most predictable woman in Nye County, and every single minute of that predictability was load-bearing.

The other life fit inside a shoebox. A Moto G, seventy dollars at a Walmart in Vegas, activated on a prepaid SIM bought with cash at a different Walmart on a different day, which never once came home powered on. Signal on it, with disappearing messages set to twenty-four hours, and no cloud backup, and a lock screen that showed nothing. That phone lived inside a Mission Darkness faraday bag inside the housing of a broken Shark vacuum in the shed, which she had gutted and rebuilt into a hollow shell with the motor gone and the switch still wired to a little fan, so that if he ever picked the thing up it would rattle and hum and feel exactly like a dead vacuum a woman had been meaning to take to the dump for two years. The ThinkPad still booted Tails off the USB stick.

For the first six months she ran with no persistent volume at all, on purpose, having decided the risk of a stick being found and imaged was worse than the daily inconvenience of setting everything up fresh, and later, when the coursework made that impossible, she made one, and made the passphrase eleven words out of a Diceware list, and committed it to memory the way she had committed the Shema to memory, which was by saying it out loud in the car. The stick rode in a tampon box in the bottom of her purse, which she noted with some grim humor was the single most reliable piece of operational security available to a woman in America. Sparrow taught her the discipline of the thing over encrypted text across those months, in fragments, at one in the morning: never sync anything, never let a device auto-connect to the house wifi, never use the same handle in two places, never explain a change in your own behavior unless you are asked, and if you are asked, keep the answer under six words.

Misha shipped her a padded envelope with a Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W, a spare AR9271, forty jumper wires, and a note that said no return address on purpose. build something. — M.

CHAPTER EIGHT

The File

That autumn she learned Python properly, because bash had taken her as far as bash takes anybody and she had started running into walls she could feel the shape of. She worked Automate the Boring Stuff the way she had worked Bandit, slowly, in a composition book, out loud, and she wept at for loops the way people weep at loops, on about the fourth day, when it finally landed that she had just told a machine to do a thing eleven thousand times and it had simply gone and done it without asking her to justify herself. Then she came at it sideways, the way she came at everything, because she had figured out that she only ever learned a thing she actually needed.

She wrote a script with requests and BeautifulSoup that pulled Nye County property records off a public portal so she would stop paying eleven dollars a look for documents that were already hers. She wrote one that walked a folder of photographs, dumped their EXIF with exiftool into a CSV with a proper timestamp column, and sorted them by date, because she had four hundred photographs of a marriage and needed them in order.

She got into scapy and spent a January weekend inside her own home network learning more about DHCP and ARP than any course would have taught her, and in the middle of that weekend she discovered a small ugly truth about the router in her own living room, which was that its administrative password had never been changed off the sticker, and she thought about that for a long time and left it alone. She built a Pi Zero into a battery-powered box that did exactly one useful thing, which was log every wireless network it saw with a timestamp, and she ran it in the van for three weeks purely because she wanted to know if she could.

By spring she could read somebody else's Python and tell inside a minute whether it was garbage, which is the milestone nobody tells you about and the only one that actually means you have arrived.

And she built the file. This is the part she is proudest of, and it is not a hacking story, it is a paperwork story, and the two things are closer relatives than anyone outside the field understands. Every incident got an entry, in a plain text file, in a folder, with a naming convention: date, time, location, what was said as near to verbatim as she could reconstruct within the hour, who else was in the house, where each child was, and what she did afterward. Photographs with a tape measure in the frame, originals preserved, EXIF intact, never once cropped or edited or run through a filter, because a defense attorney will spend an afternoon on a cropped photograph and eleven seconds on an unedited one. Screenshots of texts taken with the full header visible, exported and hashed with sha256sum, the hashes logged in a separate file, so that a timestamp could not be argued with by a man who was very good at arguing.

A Google Voice number nobody knew about, forwarding nowhere, receiving nothing. Scans of the tax returns, the mortgage, the truck title, the insurance cards, four birth certificates, the marriage license, the kids' immunization records, Marisol's IEP, and the mine's benefit paperwork, all of it done at the library at ten cents a page across five months, twenty pages at a time, and uploaded to an account with a password no living person had and a hardware key on her keyring that looked like a car fob and that Dale had asked about exactly once and been told was a loyalty tag for the feed store. Hex's people had told her the goal in one sentence: on the day you walk into a courthouse, you want to be the only person in the room who is not improvising. In April, over nothing, over a light left on, he took her wrist in the kitchen and squeezed it hard enough to leave four marks and told her she was hysterical and that the children would choose him.

Lilian photographed her own forearm in the bathroom mirror at 11:40 that night with a tape measure, uploaded it, wrote the entry, closed the laptop, pulled the USB stick, put it in the purse, and then went and lay down next to him and slept, or did not sleep, for eight hours, because it was not time yet and the money was not ready and the lease was not signed. That is the hardest thing she has ever done, and no part of it will appear on a resume.

The Paper and the Money

CHAPTER NINE

Term One, Term Two, Term Three

Western Governors University runs on six-month terms at a flat rate, which means the fast get a discount and the slow get a lesson, and Lilian Marchetti Brandt did four courses in her first term and eight in her second. She enrolled in her own name with a mailing address that was a post office box in Pahrump costing ninety-six dollars a year, and every piece of mail from that university and from the credit union went there and nowhere else, and she checked it Tuesdays on the way to the library because the post office was on the way to the library which was on the way to the groceries, and the tag in the van never had a single question to ask about any of it. She studied from nine-thirty at night until one in the morning, and ten to eleven-thirty on Tuesdays and Thursdays at the library, and in twenty-minute increments in a parked minivan outside a church preschool with a textbook flat on the steering wheel.

She passed CompTIA A+ in six weeks, Network+ in five, and Security+ in four, because by then she had worked out the thing that takes most people a year to work out, which is that a certification exam is a genre, with conventions and a house style and a set of answers it wants to hear, and Lilian had spent twenty years becoming excellent at reading a genre. Her program mentor was a man in Utah named Doug who called every other Tuesday at four, and at the nine-month mark he told her she was inside the top two percent of pace he had seen in eight years of doing the job, and then he was quiet for a second and said, are you okay? She said she was fine. Doug, to his eternal credit, said, okay. I'm going to ask you again in two weeks, and then he did, and then he kept doing it, and a year and a half later he was the second person she told.

The money came in five streams and not one of them ever touched the joint account. She bought dead ThinkPads off marketplace listings for twenty and thirty dollars, replaced thermal paste and fans and RAM and batteries and the occasional keyboard, put a clean Linux install on them with LibreOffice and a browser, and sold them for a hundred and fifty to homeschool parents and retirees and one very grateful man who ran a bail bonds office, twenty-two of them across fourteen months, and she got so fast at an X220 teardown that she could do one in front of the TV. She hardened WordPress sites for a dentist, two nail salons, a landscaper, and a horse boarding operation out toward the highway, which in practice meant killing XML-RPC, forcing two-factor on every account, setting up real off-site backups, replacing an eight-year-old contact form plugin that had a public exploit, and deleting eleven abandoned plugins nobody had opened since 2018.

She charged two hundred a site because Sparrow had told her flatly, in writing, that eighty was insulting and that undercharging is a security risk to the client because it guarantees you rush. She taught a free six-week Intro to Python at the library on Saturday mornings, which paid nothing and produced three paying referrals and one fifteen-year-old girl who is now at UNLV. And she sold cannoli at the Pahrump farmers market on Saturdays, forty dollars a week, shells fried Friday night and filled to order on the tailgate, her grandmother's ricotta cut with mascarpone, which Nonna Concetta would have called a crime against Sicily and then eaten four of. On the last Thursday of every month she drove to a credit union in Henderson and deposited cash into an account in her name only, paperless statements, no debit card mailed to any address, and she watched a number climb the way her uncles had watched a barometer.

Eleven thousand four hundred and sixty dollars. That was the number on the last Thursday in May, and she sat in the parking lot and looked at it on a phone that lived inside a vacuum cleaner and ran the arithmetic she had been running for two years, itemized, in the composition book, in pen. First, last, and deposit on a three-bedroom in Henderson: about five thousand two hundred. A twenty-foot U-Haul, a dolly, and furniture pads: two hundred and ten. Utility deposits and a new internet install: six hundred. A retainer for a temporary protective order and an emergency custody filing: two thousand five hundred, which she already knew to the dollar because she had already had the consultation, at nine in the morning on a Tuesday in April, in an office on South Fourth Street in Las Vegas, having told the tag in the van that she was taking Bennie to a pediatric dentist, which she also actually did, at eleven, forty minutes later, because a cover story that is true cannot be broken.

She had walked into that office with a thumb drive and a printed index and four hundred and six pages, organized chronologically, cross-referenced, with a hash manifest at the front. The attorney, a woman named Ruth Ames who had done family law in Clark County for twenty-two years, read the index and then read eleven pages at random out of the middle, and then set her pen down flat on the desk and looked up and said, how long have you been building this? And Lilian said, two years, and Ruth Ames said, Mrs. Brandt, I have clients who come in here with a text message and a bruise and I fight like hell for them and I lose. I have never once had this.

She interviewed in June. Tier 1 SOC analyst, managed detection and response, an office off Green Valley Parkway in Henderson, four on and four off on twelve-hour shifts with a differential for nights, which is a schedule most people flee and which Lilian looked at and recognized as the exact shape of her own life for eleven years, and understood as an advantage nobody else applying would have. Sparrow was two levels up in a different pod and recused herself from the loop entirely and told Lilian to her face, I am not putting a thumb on this. You do not want to walk in owing anybody anything, and you especially don't want to hear it in a hallway in year two. The technical was two hours. They put a pcap in front of her and asked what she saw, and she talked them through a beaconing pattern with fixed jitter and said she would want the JA3 hash and the domain registration age before she was willing to call it anything at all.

They handed her an alert and asked how she would triage it and she asked six questions before she touched the keyboard, which is the actual answer to that question and the reason most people fail it. They asked her about a time she had to explain something technical to a non-technical person and she talked about teaching an eleven-year-old to tin a soldering tip. And at the end the hiring manager asked why cybersecurity, and Lilian, who had rehearsed a perfectly serviceable lie about a lifelong passion for technology, heard herself say: Because I spent eleven years living somewhere I couldn't verify anything, and it turns out this is an entire profession of people who refuse to accept things on faith. Nobody said anything for a second.

The offer came Friday: sixty-eight thousand plus differential, start the second Monday in August. She signed a lease on the ninth in her name only, on a three-bedroom with a wall in the kitchen where a table could go, and she did not tell one living soul inside that house.

CHAPTER TEN

Eight Hours

His truck backed out of the driveway at 5:41 in the morning on a Thursday in the second week of August, and Lilian stood at the kitchen window with a cup of coffee she did not drink and watched the taillights go down Manse Road and turn left, and she counted slowly to sixty, and then she went out to the shed and took a phone out of a vacuum cleaner and sent one word: GO. The U-Haul was at the end of the street at 6:04, because Misha had driven down from Reno the night before and slept in the cab of it in a Walmart lot in Vegas. Sparrow's F-150 came in behind it towing a utility trailer, off a night shift, still in her work fleece, with two hundred dollars of coffee. Trish arrived in a Highlander with four flat-packed wardrobe boxes, three dozen breakfast burritos, a cordless impact driver, and a woman named Ronda from Operation Safe Escape who did essentially nothing for eight and a half hours except stand in the driveway with a clipboard and a phone and keep a written log of every minute of it, because if any of this ever got argued in front of a judge, somebody neutral needed to be able to say exactly what happened and exactly when.

Lilian had a laminated sheet on the refrigerator with the order of operations on it and a Sharpie tied to it with kitchen twine, and she had rehearsed the whole sequence in her head so many hundreds of times that when it finally started she was not frightened, she was bored, which is what preparation buys you and the only thing it buys you. She had a second sheet in her back pocket that Ruth Ames had written, which said in block capitals: DO NOT ENGAGE. DO NOT ANSWER THE PHONE. IF HE ARRIVES, EVERYONE LEAVES AND WE FILE. He did not arrive. The order held. The mine is sixty-two miles away and he was on shift, which was the entire reason it was that Thursday and not any other day in the history of the world.

The order was documents, then children's irreplaceables, then beds, then kitchen, then everything else, then nothing. Documents went first and they went in a single banker's box into Lilian's own van rather than the truck, because trucks get delayed and vans do not, and because a woman who has spent two years building a file does not put the file in somebody else's vehicle. Then Antonio's blinking r00tz badge, and Ruthie's rabbit, and Bennie's blanket, and the flat box of Marisol's sketchbooks, all four of those into the van too, all of it inside the first eleven minutes, because Lilian had decided a long time back that if this thing went sideways at hour six she was still going to drive away from that house with the four objects her children would grieve.

Then beds, because a child who sleeps in her own bed on the first night has moved, and a child who sleeps on a floor has fled, and Lilian had made up her mind that her children were going to move. Misha broke down four bed frames with the impact driver in thirty-five minutes and labeled every bag of hardware with the kid's name on masking tape. The kitchen took ninety minutes and Nonna's fourteen-quart pot went into a laundry basket wrapped in bath towels and rode in the footwell of the passenger seat where Lilian could see it. Marisol worked like a longshoreman, wordless, methodical, hauling boxes a size above her weight class and refusing help twice, and she did not say one thing until eleven-thirty, when she came out of her own emptied bedroom and stood in the hall and looked back at four bare walls and a rectangle of unfaded paint where her dresser had been, and said, okay. And Lilian said, okay. And that was the entire exchange, and both of them understood every word of it.

There were two runs, out of the valley on 160, up over Mountain Springs and down into the basin, sixty-two miles each way, and the second truck rolled out of Pahrump at 12:50 in the afternoon with Antonio in the middle seat because he had asked, formally, in advance, to ride in the big truck. What she left behind was as deliberate as what she took. Every stick of furniture he had bought. The television. His entire half of the closet, untouched, hung, ironed, because she was not going to hand any attorney alive a photograph of a trashed house or a scene she could be made to look small in. On the kitchen counter, in the exact center, she left three things. The Apple tag from behind the spare tire cover, which she had carried in the van all day on purpose so that its little map would show him a woman running errands in Pahrump, and which she took out at 11:15 and set down on the tile, still chirping, still working, still faithfully reporting its position, which was here, which was over.

The family-managed phone, powered on, plugged in, location on, unlocked, with eleven years of a marriage still sitting in it and not one thing deleted. And a single sheet of paper that read: The children and I are safe and we are not coming back. Do not come to my work. All communication goes through Ames & Vasquez, number below. There is a hearing on the 19th and you will be served today. — L. She had written eleven drafts of that note across four months and the final one contained no adjectives at all. Ronda's log says the last box came off the truck in Henderson at 2:38 p.m. and the locksmith finished at 3:10. The process server made contact with Dale at the mine gate in Amargosa Valley at 4:15, which was chosen precisely so that he would be sixty-two miles away, on shift, surrounded by his own coworkers, with the ex parte temporary protective order already granted that morning by a judge in Clark County who had read four hundred and six pages overnight and asked Ruth Ames exactly one question, which was whether the photographs still carried their metadata.

Eight hours and thirty-four minutes, driveway to deadbolt. That night Lilian stood in a kitchen she had never cooked in, with the sun going down behind the Spring Mountains on the wrong side of the window now, and she braided a challah on a counter with no history on it and lit two candles at 7:31. Bennie asked if Daddy was coming, and she said, no, baby, and he said okay and asked for more bread. Misha ate four cannoli standing up. Sparrow fell asleep on the couch in her boots at eight-fifteen. And Marisol, at the counter, unprompted, said the blessing, and got half of it wrong, and nobody corrected her.

She badged in for her first shift the following Monday at 6:45 in the evening and was handed a queue, a mentor, and a mug. Six weeks in she escalated a true positive that three more senior people had closed as benign, a service account authenticating from two continents inside eleven minutes at a property management client, and the incident report that came out of it went to the client with her name on the front of it, and she printed a copy and put it in the composition book. Doug from WGU called on a Tuesday in September and she told him she was fine, and this time it was true, and he was quiet for a second and said, yeah — I can hear it. She finished the degree in January, twenty-two months from enrollment, four terms, which made her the cheapest security hire in that building by a wide margin and a fact she has mentioned in exactly one salary negotiation, successfully.

The temporary order became a two-year order. Custody went where the file said it would go. The composition book, the ninety-nine-cent marbled one that spent fourteen months under forty pounds of dog food in a shed, sits on a shelf in Henderson between a DEF CON badge and a blinking circuit board that an eleven-year-old soldered himself, and every so often one of the kids takes it down and reads a page of it and puts it back. The following August she stood at the front of Salon C at the Diana Initiative and gave a twenty-minute talk called Threat Modeling Your Own House, and she opened it by holding up a sheet of paper with a block of ciphertext on it and saying, three years ago I got into this room by reading the door. Afterward a woman waited until the room had emptied out, the way they do, and came up and started talking about her sister, and then stopped, and then started again about herself.

Lilian pulled out a chair and sat down and did not look at the time. AFTERWORD FOR PRACTITIONERS

Threat modeling your own house

Everything Lilian does in this book is legal, checkable, and boring on purpose. She never accesses an account that is not hers. She never removes the tracker until the day she leaves, because switching surveillance off is itself a transmission. Her escape is a paperwork operation with an opsec wrapper — and that is the whole argument of the story.

01

Surveillance you know about is a channel

A cage you can see can be fed. Ten months of being the most predictable woman in Nye County was load-bearing infrastructure, not passivity.

02

Evidence beats catharsis

Originals preserved, EXIF intact, nothing cropped, hashes logged in a separate file. A defense attorney spends an afternoon on an edited photograph and eleven seconds on an unedited one.

03

The community is the exploit

A resume review at a con, a padded envelope with no return address, a neutral witness with a clipboard on moving day. Nobody gets out of a smiling house alone.

04

Hire the woman with the composition book

Twenty years of domestic OSINT, pattern-of-life analysis and adversary reading is a resume. If your pipeline can’t see it, the pipeline is the defect.
